# Don't Sign a Car Lease, Phone Contract or Gym Membership Until You Know This

We're often told that paying monthly is the sensible way to afford the things we need. But if you're about to sign a car lease, a phone contract or a gym membership, don't sign anything until you understand how these businesses actually make their money. I'm a chartered accountant and experienced tax adviser, and in this video I'm going to explain why the monthly price is the least useful number you'll be shown, the clauses that cost people the most, and when signing up can genuinely make sense.

On the face of it, all three of these look completely different. One is a car, one is a phone and one is a treadmill. But they're built on the same business model. They all want you on a long contract, they all want you focused on a small, comfortable monthly number, and a lot of their profit comes from what happens after you've stopped paying attention. That's things like charges at the end of the agreement, fees for leaving early and, most importantly, people who just keep paying because they forget to cancel. The industry has a name for that. It's called inertia, and it's worth a lot of money.

So let's start with the biggest commitment, which is the car lease. If you lease a car personally, that's usually called personal contract hire. You pay a monthly amount, you never own the car, and you hand it back at the end. And the headline price is often something like £299 a month. But that's rarely what you actually pay. Most leases have an initial rental, which is a lump sum upfront that's usually a multiple of the monthly payment. So you'll see deals advertised as three plus 35, six plus 35 or nine plus 35.

Let's say that you take a three-year lease at £299 a month on a nine plus 35 basis. That means you pay nine months upfront, which is £2,691, and then 35 monthly payments after that. In total, that's 44 payments, which comes to £13,156 over three years. Spread across 36 months, that's actually around £365 a month, not £299. And that's before any admin fee, which can easily be a couple of hundred pounds.

Then there are the charges at the end. Every lease has a mileage limit, and if you go over it, you pay for every extra mile. So let's say that your contract allows 8,000 miles a year, but you end up driving 10,000. Over three years, that's 6,000 extra miles, and at 10p a mile, that's another £600. And when you hand the car back, it's inspected for damage beyond fair wear and tear. Scuffed alloys, a dent in the door, a chip in the windscreen, and each of those can come with a bill.

And finally, there's leaving early. If your circumstances change and you need to get out of the lease, the early termination charge can be as much as half of all the remaining payments. So a lease is really a fixed commitment for the full term, and you should treat it that way. The recent car finance commission scandal is also a good reminder that the person selling you the finance isn't always neutral. So always look at the total cost of the agreement, not just the monthly payment.

Next is your phone contract. The traditional phone contract bundles two things together, which is the handset and your airtime, so your calls, texts and data. And the problem is that you can't easily see how much you're paying for each of them. So let's look at an example. Say you take a 24-month contract at £55 a month with a new phone included. Over two years, that's £1,320. Now say you bought the same phone outright for £899 and took a SIM-only deal at £12 a month. That's £288 for the airtime, so £1,187 in total. That's around £130 saved, just by separating the two.

But here's the thing. The bigger cost often comes after the contract ends. Once your minimum term is over, the phone is paid off, but on some older bundled contracts, the monthly price doesn't automatically come down. So people carry on paying for a handset that they already own, sometimes for years. Providers do now have to tell you when your contract is ending and what the best deals are, but they can't make you act on it.

It's also worth saying that the rules on price rises have changed. Since January 2025, Ofcom has banned phone and broadband providers from using those inflation-linked price rises in the middle of a contract, the ones that used to be something like inflation plus 3.9%. Any price rise now has to be set out in pounds and pence when you sign. So read that section of the contract, because it tells you exactly what you'll be paying in year two. And if you're already out of contract, don't just leave it. Call your provider and ask what they can do, or simply move to a SIM-only deal, because you can usually keep your number and your phone.

And one more thing. A lot of providers now split the contract into a device plan and an airtime plan. That can be more transparent, but the device plan is usually a credit agreement, which means it can show up on your credit file. So that's something to be aware of if you're about to apply for a mortgage.

Third is the gym membership. And this is the purest example of the inertia model, because a lot of gyms rely on members who pay every month but rarely go. So the number you want to look at isn't the monthly fee, it's the cost per visit. If your membership is £40 a month and you go eight times, that's £5 a visit, which is great value. But if you only go twice, that's £20 a visit, and you'd probably be better off with a pay-as-you-go pass or a class pack.

Before you sign, check three things. First, the minimum term, because a lot of cheaper deals lock you in for 12 months. Second, the notice period, because even when you're allowed to cancel, you might have to give a full month's notice, which means one more payment. And third, whether you can freeze your membership if you're injured, travelling or just going through a busy period. There are also new subscription rules under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act which are designed to make it easier to cancel and to remind you before a contract renews. But don't rely on that alone. Check what your contract actually says. And be careful with paying for a year upfront to get a discount. It only saves you money if you're genuinely going to keep going, and if the gym closes or you move, getting a refund can be difficult.

Now, let me cover a couple of mistakes to avoid across all three. The first mistake is judging a deal on the monthly price. A lower monthly payment can simply mean a bigger upfront fee, a longer term, or a big charge at the end. So always work out the total cost over the full contract, including the upfront payment and any fees. The second mistake is letting contracts roll over. The day you sign anything, put a reminder in your calendar for a month before it ends. It takes about 30 seconds, and it can save you hundreds of pounds a year.

Having said all of this, there are times when signing up can make more sense than paying upfront. If your employer offers an electric car salary sacrifice scheme, a lease can be one of the most tax-efficient ways to drive a new car. That's because the payments come out of your salary before income tax and national insurance, and the benefit in kind rate on fully electric cars is still very low. Let's say that you're a higher rate taxpayer and the lease costs £500 a month. Because that comes out of your salary before tax, you save 40% income tax and 2% national insurance, so it's really costing you around £290. You do pay benefit in kind tax on the car, and for a fully electric car with a list price of £45,000, the rate is 4% this tax year. That's a taxable benefit of £1,800, which costs you £720 a year in tax, or about £60 a month. So overall, you're paying around £350 a month for a car that would cost you £500 out of your take-home pay. Just remember that sacrificing salary can affect things like your mortgage affordability and some workplace benefits, and the benefit in kind rate on electric cars is scheduled to keep going up over the next few years. And if a phone or a car comes with genuine 0% finance and you were going to buy it anyway, keeping your cash in a savings account earning interest rather than paying everything upfront can work in your favour, as long as you make every payment on time.

So remember, don't look at the monthly price on its own. Work out the total cost, read the exit terms before you need them, and set a reminder before every contract ends. Thank you for watching. See you next time.
